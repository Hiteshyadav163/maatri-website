Basic Newborn Training

Everything you need to confidently care for your newborn from day one — safety, comfort, hygiene, and communication.

Safe Handling

🤲

Supporting the Head

  • Always cradle the head and neck — newborns have no neck muscle control
  • Use one hand under the head, one under the bottom
  • Move slowly and deliberately — no sudden jerks
  • Ensure the head is never left unsupported
🛁

Sponge Bath Basics

  • Give sponge baths until the umbilical stump falls off (7–14 days)
  • Use lukewarm water and gentle fragrance-free cleanser
  • Clean from cleanest areas to least (face → body → diaper area)
  • Keep baby wrapped and expose only the area you're washing
🩺

Diapering

  • Change every 2–3 hours or immediately after bowel movements
  • Wipe front to back (especially for girls)
  • Let the skin air-dry before closing the diaper
  • Fold diaper below the cord stump for airflow

Understanding Newborn Cues

🍼

Hunger Cues

  • Early: Rooting, turning head, mouthing movements
  • Mid: Sucking on hands or fingers
  • Late: Crying — try to feed before this stage
  • Offer the breast every 2–3 hours in the newborn period
😴

Sleep Cues

  • Yawning, eye rubbing, glazed eyes
  • Decreased activity, slowing movements
  • Turning away from stimulation
  • Arching back — ready to sleep NOW
😣

Discomfort Cues

  • Pulling up legs (gas pain)
  • Furrowing brow, grimacing
  • Persistent, high-pitched crying
  • Clenched fists and stiffened body

Burping Techniques

💆

Over the Shoulder

Hold baby upright against your chest, their chin resting on your shoulder. Use a burp cloth. Pat or rub the back gently in upward strokes. Best for: After feeding.

🧘

Sitting on Lap

Sit baby on your lap, lean them slightly forward. Support the chin and chest with one hand. Pat the back with the other. Best for: Gassy, fussy babies.

🤸

Face-Down on Lap

Lay baby face-down across your knees, supporting the head. Gently pat or rub in upward circles. Best for: When other methods aren't working.

💡
Expert Tip — Burping

Burp your baby midway through a feeding (after one breast) and again at the end. If baby seems uncomfortable and won't burp after 5 minutes, try a different position. Not all babies burp every time — that's okay!

Safe Sleep Practices

✓ Do This

  • Place baby on their back (ABCs of safe sleep)
  • Use a firm, flat sleep surface
  • Keep crib free of bumpers, toys, pillows
  • Room-share (not bed-share) for the first 6 months
  • Keep room temperature 68–72°F (20–22°C)
  • Use a sleep sack instead of loose blankets

✗ Avoid This

  • Never let baby sleep on their stomach or side
  • Avoid soft surfaces like couches or armchairs
  • No loose bedding, cords, or dangling items near crib
  • Avoid overheating — check baby's neck for sweat
  • Never leave baby unattended on elevated surfaces

Breastfeeding Positions

Finding the right position makes latching easier, reduces pain, and creates a comfortable feeding experience for both you and your baby.

🤱
Most Common

Cradle Hold

Baby's head rests in the crook of your arm on the same side as the feeding breast. Their body faces yours, tummy-to-tummy. Your other hand supports the breast.


Best for: Older babies with good head control; mothers who have recovered from birth.

🤲
Great for Newborns

Cross-Cradle Hold

Use the opposite arm to hold baby — if feeding on the right breast, use your left arm. This gives you more control over baby's head positioning and latch.


Best for: Newborns, babies with latch difficulties, small or premature babies.

🏈
Post C-Section Ideal

Football Hold

Tuck baby under your arm like a football. Their legs extend behind you, body along your forearm, face upward toward your breast. Use a pillow for arm support.


Best for: C-section moms, large breasts, premature babies, twins.

🌙
Nighttime Favourite

Side-Lying Position

Lie on your side with baby facing you, tummy-to-tummy. Align baby's mouth with your nipple. Use a rolled towel to support baby's back if needed.


Best for: Night feeds, post-C-section recovery, sore nipples, tired mothers.

Common Mistakes & How to Fix Them

😬

Shallow Latch

Baby only takes the nipple, not the areola — causes pain and reduces milk flow.

Fix: Unlatch gently with a finger, re-position so baby takes a large mouthful including the lower areola. Baby's nose should be near (not pressed into) the breast.

🪑

Poor Posture

Hunching forward causes back and neck pain for the mother.

Fix: Bring baby to the breast — not breast to baby. Use a nursing pillow to raise baby. Sit upright in a supportive chair with feet flat on the floor.

🔄

Not Switching Sides

Feeding from only one side can cause uneven supply and engorgement.

Fix: Alternate the starting breast at each feeding. Use a safety pin on your bra strap or a nursing app to track which side was used last.

💗
Tips for C-Section Mothers
  • The football hold keeps baby off the incision site — highly recommended
  • Side-lying position also avoids pressure on your abdomen
  • Use a nursing pillow as a shield across your lap for comfort
  • Request skin-to-skin in the recovery room when possible — it triggers your milk-making hormones
  • Ask for a lactation consultant visit within the first 24 hours after delivery

Breastfeeding Twins

Yes, you absolutely can breastfeed two babies! With the right setup and support, tandem feeding is achievable and incredibly bonding.

Your body is remarkable. It can produce enough milk for twins — the key is frequent, effective feeding on both sides. Your supply will meet your babies' demand.

— Certified Lactation Consultant, IBCLC

Tandem Feeding Positions

🏈🏈

Double Football Hold

Both babies tucked under your arms like footballs — one on each side. A twin nursing pillow makes this much easier.

  • Best for newborns and C-section recovery
  • Gives you visual control of both babies
  • Works well with a Boppy Twin pillow
🤱🤱

Parallel Hold

Both babies in cradle hold, facing the same direction. One baby lies on top of the other's legs.

  • Comfortable for mothers with larger laps
  • Easier once babies have good head control
  • A large nursing pillow is essential
✖️

Criss-Cross Hold

Babies lie across each other, each in a cradle position. Their bodies form an X across your lap.

  • Great for older, larger babies
  • Good if one baby has a stronger latch
  • Allows you to use your arms more freely
🔁

Alternating / Sequential

Feed one baby at a time. Pump the other breast while feeding, or feed one then the other.

  • Best when one twin needs extra help latching
  • Allows more focused attention per baby
  • More time-consuming but perfectly valid

Scheduling vs. On-Demand Feeding

📅 Scheduled Feeding

  • Feed both twins at the same time, every 2–3 hours
  • When one wakes to feed, wake the other too
  • Helps create a predictable routine for exhausted parents
  • Maintains equal supply stimulation on both sides
  • Recommended by most twin lactation consultants

🍼 On-Demand Feeding

  • Feed each baby when they show hunger cues
  • More natural but harder to manage with twins
  • Perfectly fine if one twin feeds more frequently
  • Can lead to fatigue if twins have very different schedules
  • Works well once breastfeeding is well established (6–8 weeks)

Managing Milk Supply for Twins

⭐
Quick Tips for Maintaining Supply
  • Feed or pump at least 8–12 times per 24 hours in the early weeks
  • Assign each baby to one breast consistently — this regulates supply per baby's demand
  • Pump after feeds to fully drain breasts and stimulate more production
  • Stay well hydrated — aim for 3+ litres of water daily
  • Eat an additional 500–600 calories above your pre-pregnancy needs
  • Rest when babies rest — fatigue significantly reduces milk supply

Common Challenges & Solutions

😩

Exhaustion

Accept all offers of help. Prepare snacks and water before every feed. Consider a night nurse or partner night shifts for bottle feeding pumped milk.

⚖️

Unequal Supply

If one side produces less, nurse the weaker feeder on that side more often, or pump more frequently on that side to increase stimulation.

😰

Latch Differences

Work with each twin individually at first. A stronger feeder on a weaker side can help draw milk out. Ask your lactation consultant for a nipple shield if needed.

📊

Feeding Logs & Tracking

Keeping a simple record of feeds helps you understand your baby's patterns, reassures you that feeding is going well, and flags concerns early.

Why Tracking Matters

📋

Catch Issues Early

Logs reveal if feeds are too short, too infrequent, or if diaper output drops — giving you time to act before problems escalate.

🔁

Track Supply & Patterns

Know which side was fed last, how long feeds lasted, and whether baby is feeding at least 8 times in 24 hours.

🩺

Doctor's Visits

Your pediatrician will ask about feeds and diaper counts. A log saves you from guessing and builds confidence in your answers.

Digital Tracking Apps

Apps like Huckleberry, Baby Tracker, or Glow Baby make logging easy from your phone. Many let you share logs with your partner or midwife. A simple notebook works just as well!

Signs Baby Is Getting Enough Milk

✅

Reassuring Signs

  • At least 6 wet diapers per day after day 4
  • Yellow, seedy, or watery stools (breastfed babies)
  • Gaining 150–200g (5–7 oz) per week after day 5
  • Baby appears satisfied after feeds
  • Breast feels softer after feeding
  • You can hear baby swallowing during feeds
📈

Normal Weight Pattern

  • Babies typically lose 5–7% of birth weight in first days
  • Should regain birth weight by 10–14 days
  • From day 5 onwards: steady 150–200g/week gain
  • Weigh at your midwife or pediatric visits
🚨
Red Flags — Consult Your Healthcare Provider
  • Fewer than 3–4 wet diapers per day after day 4
  • Baby still hasn't regained birth weight by 2 weeks
  • Persistent dark or brick-red urine after day 3
  • Baby is very sleepy and hard to wake for feeds
  • Feeding is consistently painful for you
  • Baby seems unsatisfied after every feeding
  • You see blood in your milk or in baby's stool

Lactation Supplements & Nutrition

What you eat and drink directly impacts your milk supply and your own energy and recovery. Here's how to nourish yourself well.

Natural Ways to Boost Milk Supply

🕐

Frequent Feeding

The single most effective way to boost supply is to nurse more often. The more the breast is emptied, the faster it refills. Aim for 8–12 feeds in 24 hours in the first weeks.

💧

Hydration

Drink a glass of water every time you sit down to nurse. Breastfeeding mothers need approximately 3–3.5 litres of fluid daily. Dehydration is one of the most common causes of reduced supply.

😴

Rest & Stress Reduction

Stress and exhaustion lower prolactin levels — the milk-making hormone. Rest between feeds, accept help, and practise gentle breathing or relaxation before nursing.

Common Lactation Supplements (Galactagogues)

🌱

Fenugreek

One of the most widely used herbs. May increase supply within 24–72 hours. Some mothers notice a maple-syrup scent in sweat/milk.

Consult first if diabetic or on blood thinners
🌿

Shatavari

An Ayurvedic herb traditionally used for lactation and postpartum recovery. Generally considered safe with very few side effects.

Generally considered safe
🌾

Oats (Oatmeal)

Rich in beta-glucan, oats may help increase prolactin. Eat a bowl of oatmeal daily or add to lactation cookies.

Safe & nutritious food
🫚

Moringa

A nutrient-dense plant shown in studies to significantly increase milk volume. Available as powder or capsule. Very safe and nutritious.

Well-tolerated by most mothers
🌼

Blessed Thistle

Often combined with fenugreek for synergistic effect. Used to stimulate milk production and improve digestion.

Not recommended during pregnancy
🍞

Brewer's Yeast

Rich in B vitamins, iron, and protein. Many mothers add it to lactation cookies or smoothies for a nutrient and energy boost.

Safe for most mothers
⚠️
Safety Reminders — Before Taking Any Supplement
  • Always consult your doctor or midwife before starting any herb or supplement
  • Some herbs can cause reactions in sensitive babies (colicky behaviour, loose stools)
  • Supplements are NOT a replacement for frequent feeding and proper latch
  • If supply doesn't improve within 2 weeks, request a full lactation assessment

Daily Nutrition Recommendations

🥗

Extra Calories

Add 400–500 extra calories per day above your pre-pregnancy needs. For twins, add 800–1000 calories.

🥩

Protein

Aim for 65–71g of protein daily. Include eggs, lentils, dairy, chicken, fish, and legumes at every meal.

🐟

Omega-3 Fatty Acids

DHA supports baby's brain development. Eat 2 servings of low-mercury fish per week (salmon, sardines, trout).

🥦

Calcium & Iron

Continue your prenatal vitamin. Eat dark leafy greens, dairy, fortified cereals, and legumes to replenish stores.

☀️

Vitamin D

Breast milk is often low in Vitamin D. Supplement your own intake and ask your doctor about infant Vitamin D drops (400 IU/day).

🫐

Antioxidants

Colourful fruits and vegetables provide antioxidants that support healing and pass beneficial compounds through milk.

✓ Eat & Drink More Of

  • Water, coconut water, herbal teas, milk
  • Oatmeal, whole grains, brown rice
  • Eggs, lentils, chickpeas, tofu
  • Salmon, sardines, mackerel (low mercury)
  • Dark leafy greens (spinach, kale, fenugreek leaves)
  • Almonds, walnuts, flaxseeds
  • Lactation cookies or energy balls

✗ Reduce or Avoid

  • Alcohol (wait 2 hours per unit before nursing)
  • Excess caffeine — limit to 1–2 cups/day
  • High-mercury fish (swordfish, shark, tilefish)
  • Highly processed or fried foods
  • Spearmint or peppermint tea (can reduce supply)
  • Sage and parsley in large amounts
  • Crash dieting — not safe during breastfeeding
💛
A Word of Encouragement

Breastfeeding is a skill that both you and your baby are learning together. It may feel challenging in the beginning, and that is completely normal. Be patient with yourself. Reach out to a certified lactation consultant (IBCLC) early — they can make an enormous difference. You are doing an incredible thing for your baby. Every drop counts.
